9  Epistemic Logic

Modal logic has been used to model knowledge and belief. Epistemic logic provides a formal framework for the study of different aspects of knowledge and its interaction with belief.

9.1 Knowledge

Basic epistemic logic begins with (Hintikka 1962). The first step is to supplement the language of propositional logic with a family of propositional operators \(\textsf{K}_i\) for a family of epistemic agents \(i\in I\). A formula of the form \(\textsf{K}_i \varphi\) is read: “agent \(i\) knows that \(\varphi\),” and \(\neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi\) is read: “agent \(i\) does not know that \(\varphi\).” On the other hand, \(\textsf{K}_i \neg \varphi\) is read: “agent \(i\) knows that not-\(\varphi\),” which amounts to the claim that agent \(i\) has ruled out that \(\varphi\). The dual of \(\textsf{K}_i\), \(\neg \textsf{K}_i \neg\), is symbolized: \(\langle \textsf{K}_i \rangle\), is read: “agent \(i\) has not ruled out \(\varphi\)” or “for all agent \(i\) knows, might be the case.” If to know that not-\(\varphi\) is to rule \(\varphi\) out, then to not know that not-\(\varphi\) is to not rule \(\varphi\) out, which is to say that \(\varphi\) might, for all the agent knows, the case.

Here is the broad picture as described by Lewis (1986) in terms of epistemic alternatives:

The content of someone’s knowledge of the world is given by his class of epistemically accessible worlds. These are the worlds that might, for all he knows, be his world; world \(w\) is one of them iff he knows nothing, either explicitly or implicitly, to rule out the hypothesis that \(w\) is the world where he lives.

In what follows we speak of states rather than worlds, and we take a state \(v\) to be accessible from another state \(u\) just in case whatever the agent knows to be the case in \(u\) is the case in \(v\).

Definition 9.1 (States of Information) A relational model \(M\) for \(\mathcal{L}^{K_i}\) is a structure \((W, \{R_i: i\in I\}, V)\), where:

  1. \(W\) is a non-empty set of information states or states of the world,
  2. For each \(i\in I\), \(R_i\) is a binary accessibility relation on \(W\), and
  3. \(V\) is a function, which assigns to each propositional variable \(p\) a set of states \(V(p)\).

States encode information. In a poker game, players may be concerned with what cards other players may have, and they may therefore consider deals consistent with the cards they have received. We may think of a state in that case as given by a distribution of cards to the relevant players. As the game progresses, players may gain information allowing them to rule out certain distributions as live epistemic alternatives.

Or consider another real-life example. Suppose you have lost sight of your phone, which you think, you may have been stolen. In one state, your phone has been stolen (\(p\)), whereas in another state it has merely been misplaced but not stolen (\(\neg p\)). In all epistemically accessible states, however, your phone had been with you just yesterday night (\(q\)). That would place you in a state in which you know your phone had been with you yesterday night, \(\textsf{K}_i q\), but you do not know whether your phone has been stolen: \(\neg \textsf{K}_i p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i \neg p\).

One way to model the situation is through a model, which consists of two states \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) corresponding to two epistemic alternatives: one in which your phone has been stolen (\(p\)), and one in which it has not been stolen (\(\neg p\)).

Notice that \(\textsf{K}_i q\) is true at either state because \(q\) is true at all epistemically accessible states, yet neither \(\textsf{K}_i p\) nor \(\textsf{K}_i \neg p\) is true at either state.

Knowledge rules out epistemic alternatives and helps one locate the actual world within the space of epistemic live options. Your lack of knowledge whether your phone has been stolen is represented by your inability to locate the actual world in the space of epistemic live alternatives described above. Nothing you know enables you to distinguish \(s_1\) from \(s_2\).

We now explain what it is for a formula \(\varphi\) true at a state \(s\) in a model \(M\) of the form \((W, \{R_i: i\in I\}, V)\), written \(M, s \Vdash \varphi\), inductively.

Definition 9.2 (Truth at a State) If \(M\) is a model of the form \((W, \{R_i: i\in I\}, V)\), we define \(M, s \Vdash \varphi\) as follows:

\[\begin{array}{lll} M, s \Vdash p & \text{iff} & s \in V(p)\\ M, s \Vdash \neg \varphi & \text{iff} & M, s \nVdash \varphi\\ M, s \Vdash (\varphi \to \psi) & \text{iff} & M, s \nVdash \varphi \ \text{or} \ M, s \Vdash \psi\\ M, s \Vdash \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \text{iff} & \text{for every} \ u \in W \ \text{s.t.} \ R_i su, \ M, u \Vdash \varphi\\ \end{array}\] Since \(\langle \textsf{K}_i \rangle\) as the dual of \(\textsf{K}_i\), that is, \(\neg \textsf{K}_i \neg\), we have: \[\begin{array}{lll} M, s \Vdash \langle \textsf{K}_i \rangle \varphi & \text{iff} & \text{for some} \ u \in W \ \text{s.t.} \ R_i su, \ M, u \Vdash \varphi\\ \end{array}\]

We are now in a position to define truth in a model of epistemic logic.

Definition 9.3 (Truth in a Model) A formula \(\varphi\) is true in a model \(M\) of the form \((W, \{R_i: i\in I\}, V)\), written \(M\Vdash \varphi\), if, and only if, for every \(s\in W\), \(M, s \Vdash \varphi\).

9.1.1 Axioms for Epistemic Logic

These decisions are not inconsequential. For it follows that agents know all logical truths and that they know what follows from what they know. That is the framework vindicates a rule of necessitation and all substitution instances of axiom \(\textsf{K}\): \[\tag{\textsf{RN}} \varphi / \textsf{K}_i \varphi\]

\[\tag{\textsf{K}} \textsf{K}_i(p \to q) \to (\textsf{K}_ip \to \textsf{K}_iq)\]

This involves a modicum of idealization: ordinary knowledge is not closed under logical consequence, and it is not all that , plausible to think agents ordinarily know all logical truths.

One important consequence is that knowledge commutes with conjunction:

\[\tag{\textsf{M}} \textsf{K}_i(p \wedge q) \to (\textsf{K}_ip \wedge \textsf{K}_iq)\]

One way to cope with these observation is to let \(\textsf{K}_i\) model not ordinary knowledge but rather knowledge by an idealized agent equipped with unlimited logical capabilities. Alternatively, one may instead take \(\textsf{K}_i\) to model implicit knowledge, which is what follows from knowledge we may reasonably attribute to ordinary agents even if it is not explicitly known by them.

The next step is to consider the epistemic interpretation of different modal axioms and to ask whether or not they are candidates for the logic of knowledge.

What is known is the case.

\[\tag{\textsf{T}} \textsf{K}_i p \to p\]

The principle that knowledge implies truth is virtually uncontroversial, and a firm candidate for the logic of knowledge. From a semantic point of view, \(\textsf{T}\) modally defines the class of reflexive frames, and its substitution instances are true in all reflexive models: if \(\textsf{K}_i p\) is true in all epistemic alternatives to a given state \(s\), then since \(s\) itself is such an alternative, \(p\) is true at \(s\).

One consequence of the combination of \(\textsf{T}\) and \(\textsf{M}\), according to which knowledge commutes with conjunction, is that Moorean sentences of the form

\[(\varphi \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi)\]

become unknowable. Consider what happens if I tell you that your phone has been stolen but you do not know it, which we could formalize \(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p\). Could you ever come to know that conjunction? That is, could it be the case that \(\textsf{K}_i(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_ip)\)? Notice that the sentence is falsified at every state of the model we considered. For suppose you come to know the conjunction \(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p\): \(\textsf{K}_i(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_ip)\). By (\(\textsf{M}\)) above, you would come to know the two conjuncts: \(\textsf{K}_i p \wedge \textsf{K}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i p\). But that would give us both \(\textsf{K}_ip\) and \(\textsf{K}_i\neg \textsf{K}_ip\). By axiom \(\textsf{T}\), the latter would only be true if \(\neg \textsf{K}_ip\), which would contradict \(\textsf{K}_i p\).

What might be known is the case.

Recall that we are able to move freely from \((\textsf{B})\) to its dual \((\textsf{B}_\Diamond)\):

\[\tag{$\textsf{B}$} p \to \textsf{K}_i \langle \textsf{K}_i \rangle p\]

\[\tag{$\textsf{B}_\Diamond$} \langle \textsf{K}_i \rangle \textsf{K}_i p \to p\]

Here is a gloss of the dual: if for all \(i\) knows, \(i\) knows that \(p\), then \(p\) is the case. In other words, if it is compatible with what I know that I know \(p\), then \(p\) is in fact the case. Or, for a more compact formulation: whatever might be known is the case.

\((\textsf{B})\) corresponds to the symmetry of the \(i\)-accessibility relations: for all \(s, t \in W\), if \(R_i st\), then \(R_i ts\). (Williamson 2000) observes that symmetry plays a role in arguments for radical skepticism. For suppose that in a given state \(s\), the agent \(i\) truly believes that they have a hand. The skeptic describes an epistemic alternative \(t\) at which the agent \(i\) believes that they have a hand while being in fact a brain in a vat. It is uncontroversial that the non-skeptic state is an epistemic alternative to \(s\) in the skeptic scenario: \(Rts\). That is, all \(i\) knows at \(t\) is in fact the case at \(s\). The agent does not know, for example, that they are a brain in a vat. Given symmetry then, \(Rst\), which is to say that all \(i\) knows at \(s\) is in fact the case at \(t\). But since \(i\) does not have a hand at \(t\), it follows that, at \(s\), agent \(i\) does not know that they have a hand.

One way to undermine confidence in \((\textsf{B})\) is to note that it delivers substitution instances of the principle:

\[\neg p \to \textsf{K}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi.\]

If you fail to know something, then you know that you do not know it. The problem is that the brain in a vat neither have hands nor know that they do not know that they have hands. In other words, they do not have hands, and they do not know that they do not know.

Symmetry follows from the combination of reflexivity and the euclidean condition: if \(R_i\) is reflexive and euclidean, then \(R_i\) is symmetric. But the euclidean condition corresponds to axiom \(\textsf{5}\). To the extent to which \(\textsf{KT5}\) or \(\textsf{S5}\) extends \(\textsf{KTB}\), the above considerations extend to axiom \(\textsf{5}\), which in the presence of \(\textsf{T}\) would give us \(\textsf{B}\).

What is not ruled out is known to not be ruled out.

\[\tag{$\textsf{5}$} \langle \textsf{K}_i\rangle p \to \textsf{K}_i \langle \textsf{K}_i \rangle p\]

Consider the dual:

\[\tag{$\textsf{5}_\Diamond$} \langle \textsf{K}_i \rangle \textsf{K}_i p \to \textsf{K}_i p\]

If an agent \(i\) has not ruled out that she knows that \(p\), then she knows that \(p\). This is implausible. There are many cases in which agents mistakenly take themselves to know something that is in fact not the case. For example, I may mistakenly take myself to know that taking a certain route is the most effective way to reach my destination at a given time. Unbeknownst to me, there are special circumstances that make it the case that the rout in question is not in fact the most effective. Since knowledge entails truth, I do not know that, but I have still not ruled out that I know the proposition in question. According to a substitution instance of \(\textsf{5}\), it follows that I know the proposition when in fact I do not. In other words, \(\textsf{5}\) would require agents to be immune to error of the sort just now described, which is again quite implausible.

For another related consideration against \(\textsf{5}\), consider extensions of epistemic logic with a doxastic operator \(\textsf{B}_i\), where the condition \(\textsf{B}_i \varphi\) is read: “agent \(i\) believes that \(\varphi\).” Two attractive principles are:

\[\textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{B}_i \varphi\]

\[\tag{\textsf{D}} \textsf{B}_i \varphi \to \neg \textsf{B}_i \neg \varphi\]

The problem is that the combination of \((\textsf{5})\) with these two principles delivers an implausible consequence:

\[B_i K_ip \to K_i p\]

Proof. The key is to begin with a simple consequence of \(\textsf{5}\):

\[\begin{array}{lll} 1 & \neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{K}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \textsf{5} \\ 2 & \textsf{K}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{B}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \textsf{K}_i \to \textsf{B}_i\\ 3 & \textsf{B}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \neg \textsf{B}_i \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \textsf{D} \ \text{for} \ \textsf{B}_i\\ 4 & \textsf{B}_i \textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \textsf{PL} \ 1, 2, 3 \end{array}\]

It follows that to believe that you know is sufficient for you to know, and no one can ever mistakenly believe that the know what they in fact fail to know. That seems utterly implausible.

What is known is known to be known.

\[\tag{\textsf{4}} \textsf{K}_i p \to \textsf{K}_i \textsf{K}_i p\]

This is the principle commonly known as the \(\textsf{K}\textsf{K}\)-principle. It encodes the view that knowledge is transparent to the agent: if you know something, then you know that you know. The thought is that agents with unlimited introspection capabilities ought to be able to recognize their own knowledge: agents should be able to introspectively attend to their reasons for belief, which should be conclusive in cases of knowledge and not defeasible by new information.

From a semantic point of view, \(\textsf{4}\) requires the \(i\)-accessibility relations to be transitive: if a state \(t\) is an genuine epistemic alternative for \(i\) at \(v\), and \(v\) is an epistemic alternative for \(i\) at \(u\), then \(t\) is an epistemic alternative for \(i\) at \(u\).

Hintikka (1962) adopted axioms \(\textsf{T}\) and \(\textsf{4}\) and identified \(\textsf{S4}\) as a candidate logic for knowledge. His reasons for that is that he regarded axioms \(\textsf{B}\) and \(\textsf{5}\) as implausible epistemic principles.

On the other hand, the interaction between knowledge and belief offers some reason to be concerned. For \(\textsf{4}\) immediately delivers:

\[\textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{B}_i \textsf{K}_i \varphi\]

Proof. The argument combines \(\textsf{4}\) with the principle that knowledge entails belief:

\[\begin{array}{lll} 1 & \textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{K}_i \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \textsf{4}\\ 2 & \textsf{K}_i \textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{B}_i \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \textsf{Knowledge entails belief}\\ 3 & \textsf{K}_i \varphi \to \textsf{B}_i \textsf{K}_i \varphi & \textsf{PL} \ 1, 2 \end{array}\]

In other words, I know something only if I believe that I know it.

The most influential argument against \(\textsf{4}\) comes from Timothy Williamson’s margin of error principles in Williamson (2000, chap. 5). Suppose you glance at the crowd in a stadium and estimate the attendance. Let \(p_n\) be the proposition that the true count is \(n\). Because your eyesight has limited powers of discrimination, you cannot tell apart crowds that differ by a single person. So, a margin of error principle looks plausible:

\[\begin{equation}\tag{\textsf{MEP}} \forall n\,(\textsf{K}\neg p_n \to \neg p_{n+1}). \end{equation}\]

In words, you know the count is not \(n\) only if it is not \(n+1\) either. Here is why. Suppose the true count were \(n+1\) and you believed it was not \(n\). A crowd of \(n\) would look exactly the same to you as this crowd, which means that you would have formed the same belief if the count had been \(n\), and then it would have been false. Your belief would be true only by luck, and a belief that is true by luck cannot be knowledge.

Now suppose you are reflective enough to know the margin of error principle for yourself. in combination with \(\textsf{4}\), it yields that you know the count is not \(p_{n}\) only if you know it is not \(p_{n+1}\):

Proof. We use a known instance of \(\textsf{MEP}\):

\[\begin{array}{lll} 1 & \textsf{K}\neg p_n \to \textsf{K}\textsf{K}\neg p_n & \textsf{4}\\ 2 & \textsf{K}(\textsf{K}\neg p_n \to \neg p_{n+1}) & \text{known instance of \textsf{MEP}}\\ 3 & \textsf{K}\textsf{K}\neg p_n \to \textsf{K}\neg p_{n+1} & \textsf{K}, 2\\ 4 & \textsf{K}\neg p_n \to\textsf{K}\neg p_{n+1} & \textsf{K},\ \textsf{PL},\ 1, 3 \end{array}\]

So if you know that the true count is not \(n\), you also know it is not \(n+1\). That is unacceptable. You surely know the count is not \(0\). A simple induction would now reveal that for every number \(n\), including the true count, you know that it is not the count of people in attendance. Something must give, and Williamson’s suggestion is to blame substitution instances of axiom \(\textsf{4}\).

9.1.2 Applications

9.1.2.1 The Unexpected Examination

The paradox of the unexpected examination arises when a teacher makes the announcement that there will be an exam next term, which will come as a surprise: students will not know on the morning of the exam, that it is that day. Students reason backwards. The exam cannot be on the last day of classes, since otherwise, they would know that the exam is that day. Given that, it cannot be on the penultimate day either: they ruled out the last day, which means that if the exam has not taken place by the penultimate day, then that would be the only available option. Similar reasons rule out other days until no day is left. Yet, the teacher gives the exam and the students are surprised.

Williamson (2000, chap. 6) suggests the same issue arises in a much simpler case, which he dubs Glimpse. Students know that the teacher circles all and only examination dates on her office calendar. From a distant glimpse, they note that exactly one date is circled, and that it is not very near the end of term, but they cannot narrow it down much further. Let \(e_n\) be the claim that the exam will take place \(n\) days before the end of term. That is, \(e_0\) is the claim that the exam will take place on the last day, and \(e_1\) is the claim that the exam will take place on the penultimate day. Because the glimpse is imprecise, the students’ knowledge is subject to a margin of error principle:

\[\tag{\textsf{MEP}} \forall n \,(\textsf{K}\neg e_n \to \neg e_{n+1}).\]

The glimpse is too coarse to rule out that the exam will take place \(n\) days before the end of term if the exam does in fact take place \(n+1\) days before the end of term. Because the students understand their situation, they are aware of this principle. Furthermore, they know from the glimpse that the exam will not take place on the very last day of term: \(\textsf{K} \neg e_0\). Given axiom \(\textsf{4}\), they are able to argue:

\[\textsf{K} \neg e_0 \to \textsf{K} \neg e_1\]

Proof. The argument uses a known instance of \(\textsf{MEP}\):

\[\begin{array}{lll} 1 & \textsf{K}\neg e_0 \to \textsf{K}\textsf{K}\neg e_0 & \textsf{4}\\ 2 & \textsf{K}(\textsf{K}\neg e_0 \to \neg e_{1}) & \text{known instance of \textsf{MEP}}\\ 3 & \textsf{K}\textsf{K}\neg e_0 \to \textsf{K}\neg e_{1} & \textsf{K}, 2\\ 4 & \textsf{K}\neg e_0 \to\textsf{K}\neg e_{1} & \textsf{K},\ \textsf{PL},\ 1, 3 \end{array}\]

But once they can rule out \(e_1\), by \(\textsf{4}\), they know that they know, and a similar argument enables them to rule out \(e_2\). More generally, we have:

\[\textsf{K} \neg e_n \to \textsf{K} \neg e_{n+1}\]

Students are thus in a position to rule out \(e_{n+1}\) once they rule out \(e_n\). That means that they are, on the face of it, able to rule out every day of term, and yet the exam takes place and they are surprised. Something must give again, and Williamson suggests that the students’ error is to rely on \(\textsf{4}\).

9.1.2.2 Fitch’s paradox of knowability

Some philosophers claim that what is true never outstrips what can be known: whatever is the case can, in principle, be known. When we read \(\Diamond \varphi\) as: “\(\varphi\) is metaphysically possible”, we may regiment the principle: \[p \to \Diamond \textsf{K}_i p \tag{\textsf{Knowability}}\] Fitch’s paradox is the observation that \(\textsf{Knowability}\) entails what looks like an implausible principle: \[\tag{\textsf{Knowledge}} p \to \textsf{K}_i p,\] according to which whatever is true is known.

Recall the observation that Moorean propositions cannot be known. If, unbeknownst to you, your iphone has been stolen, then we have this: \[p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p\]This would be a perfectly ordinary truth that you could never come to know. To know the conjunction, you must come to know each conjunct, but as soon as you come to know the first conjunct, \(p\), the second conjunct, \(\neg \textsf{K}_i p\), becomes false and thus cannot be known. The formal argument requires only instances of \(\textsf{M}\) and \(\textsf{T}\): \[\begin{array}{lll} 1 & \textsf{K}_i(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p) \to (\textsf{K}_i p \wedge \textsf{K}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i p) & \textsf{M}\\ 2 & \textsf{K}_i \neg \textsf{K}_i p \to \neg \textsf{K}_i p & \textsf{T}\\ 3 & \neg \textsf{K}_i(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p) & \textsf{PL} \ 1, 2 \end{array}\]

Since the conclusion of the argument is a theorem, it depends only on logical and epistemic principles that should be available across possibilities. Given the rule of necessitation \(\textsf{RN}\) for \(\Box\), we obtain: \[\begin{array}{lll} 4 & \Box \neg \textsf{K}_i(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p) & \textsf{RN} \ 3\\ 5 & \neg \Diamond \textsf{K}_i(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p) & \textsf{Def} \ \Diamond \ 4 \end{array}\]

We are now in a position to appreciate how \(\textsf{Knowability}\) collapses into \(\textsf{Knowledge}\): \[\begin{array}{lll} 6 & (p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p) \to \Diamond \textsf{K}_i(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p) & \textsf{Knowability}\\ 7 & p \to \textsf{K}_i p & \textsf{PL} \ 5, 6 \end{array}\]

That is, if \(p\) is an unknown truth, then the Moorean proposition \(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p\) becomes unknowable. It follows that if all truths are indeed knowable, then all truths are indeed known.

Consider the issue from a semantic point of view. A model for the hybrid language requires two accessibility relations: \(R_i\) for \(i\)’s knowledge and \(R_\Diamond\) for metaphysical possibility. \(\textsf{K}_i\varphi\) is true at \(w\) iff \(\varphi\) is true at every \(R_i\)-successor of \(w\), and \(\Diamond\varphi\) is true at \(w\) iff \(\varphi\) is true at some \(R_\Diamond\)-successor of \(w\).

Two points stand out. First, the application of \(\textsf{RN}\) in line 4 presupposes that line 3 obtains not just at the world of evaluation but at all \(R_\Diamond\)-accessible worlds. Since line 3 depends on \(\textsf{T}\), this requires \(R_i\) to be reflexive at every world.

Second, and more important, \(\Diamond\) shifts the world of evaluation for the embedded formula, which includes the inner \(\neg \textsf{K}_i p\), to another \(R_\Diamond\)-accessible world. Consider a world \(w\) at which \(p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p\) is true, and a world \(v\) \(R_\Diamond\)-accessible from \(w\), where \(i\) has learned that \(p\).

\[\underset{p,\ \neg \textsf{K}_i p}{w} \quad \xrightarrow{\;R_\Diamond\;} \quad \underset{p,\ \textsf{K}_i p}{v}\]

For \(\textsf{Knowability}\) to be true at \(w\), \(\textsf{K}_i (p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p)\) must be true at some \(R_\Diamond\)-accessible world \(u\). Since knowledge of the conjunction requires knowledge of the conjuncts, this has to be a world like \(v\), where \(i\) knows \(p\). But at \(v\), the second conjunct states that \(i\) does not know \(p\) at \(v\), not at \(w\). Since knowledge is factive, \(i\) cannot know the conjunction at \(v\) or at any other world at which she knows \(p\). On the face of it, what we want is a world like \(v\), where \(i\) knows that \(p\) and knows that she did not know \(p\) at \(w\). But the formula \(\textsf{K}_i (p \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_i p)\) cannot express this, because the inner \(\textsf{K}_i\) concerns the epistemic state of \(i\) at the world of evaluation. The paradox, on this view, arises because there are truths concerned with the agent’s epistemic situation, and coming to know them makes them false.

9.2 Information Flow

In epistemic logic, agents locate themselves in the space of epistemic alternatives by ruling out certain states as genuine epistemic alternatives in light of new information.

The Muddy Children puzzle illustrates the dynamic aspect of this update mechanism:

Example 9.1 (Muddy Children) Three children \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) have been playing outside when they are called back inside by their guardian. Two of them, say \(a\) and \(c\), have mud on their forehead, but none of them can see what is on their forehead. Each child, however, can see which of the other children are muddy. Once inside, the guardian asserts:

  1. At least one of you is muddy.

followed by an initial request:

  1. If you know you are muddy, please step forward.

None of the children step forward after the first request. Each child will see other children with mud in their foreheads, but they do not yet know whether they themselves are muddy.

Matters change when she repeats the same request for a second time:

  1. If you know you are muddy, please step forward.

After the second request, the two muddy children, \(a\) and \(c\), will step forward while the other child, \(b\), stays behind. For \(a\) and \(c\) now know that they are muddy.

The example raises some questions:

  • Did the children learn anything new after the announcement that one of them was muddy? They each knew after all that some children were muddy.

  • What changed between the initial request when no one stepped forward and the later one when two muddy children did?

  • How did the muddy children \(a\) and \(c\) come to know that they had mud in their foreheads?

Before Announcement A

We represent the relevant states of information with respect to the question at hand by ordered triples of digits, which encode the state of each children in that order. Thus for example:

  • \(101\) encodes the actual state of the world on which \(a\) is muddy, \(b\) is not muddy, and \(c\) is muddy.
  • \(111\) encodes an epistemic live option for \(b\) (though not for \(a\) or \(c\)) on which all three children are muddy.
  • \(001\) encodes an epistemic live option for \(a\) (though not for \(b\) or \(c\)) on which only \(c\) is muddy.

The eight nodes correspond to eight states, which consist of different distributions of mud across the children. The nodes are connected by different accessibility relations, which correspond to each child as an epistemic agent. We assume that each relation is reflexive and symmetric: when an edge labeled \(a\), for example, connects two nodes, the two states are indistinguishable as far as \(a\) is concerned.

The effect of the announcement \(A\) is to let the children exclude \(000\) as a live epistemic option.

After Announcement A

While announcement \(A\) excludes \(000\) as actuality, none of the children regarded \(000\) as an epistemic live option. That is because each children is able to see mud in the forehead of at least one child.

The children learn valuable information from the fact that no one steps forward after the first request, \(B\), is made. This is because the children now learn that none of the other two children know that they are muddy. This suffices to rule out states at which there is exactly one muddy child.

After Request B

After the second request, \(C\), \(a\) and \(c\) will simultaneously step forward since they find no epistemically open alternative at which they are not muddy. On the other hand, \(b\) will stay back because she cannot tell which of \(101\) or \(111\) are actual. That is, \(b\) stays back because \(b\) cannot rule out that she is muddy.

That allows the children to eliminate every epistemic alternative to \(101\). So, the epistemic situation after the second request is that the children know exactly who is muddy and who is not muddy.

After Request C

Nothing changes when we complicate the situation. If, for example, seven out of ten children happened to be muddy, then no one would step forward until after the guardian makes the seventh request at which point exactly those seven children would simultaneously step forward.

The diagrams suggest a simple update mechanism. Once a truth \(\varphi\) is announced, agents are in a position to eliminate states at which \(\varphi\) fails.

Definition 9.4 (Update) If \(M = (W, \{R_i: i\in I\}, V)\) and \(\varphi\) is a formula, the update of \(M\) by \(\varphi\) is the model \(M|\varphi = (W', \{R'_i: i \in I\}, V')\), where:

  1. \(W' = \{s \in W : M, s \Vdash \varphi\}\),

  2. \(R'_i = R_i \cap (W' \times W')\), and

  3. \(V'(p) = V(p) \cap W'\)

We now add an operator \([!\varphi]\), where: \([!\varphi]\psi\) is read: “after the announcement of \(\varphi\), \(\psi\). Its truth clause is:

\[\begin{array}{lll} M, s \Vdash [!\varphi] \psi & \text{iff} & M, s \Vdash \varphi \ \text{only if} \ M|\varphi, s \Vdash \psi\\ \end{array}\]

The antecedent reflects the assumption that announcements are true.

To return to the Muddy Children case, let \(p_i\) be the proposition that child \(i\) is muddy with \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are children \(1\), \(2\), and \(3\), respectively. Then, \(A\) is the announcement:

\[p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3\]

The effect of the announcement is to remove the state \(000\), which is exactly the transition from the first to the second diagram. Now, the failure to step forward after \(B\) is itself informative as it amounts to the public announcement that no child knows that they are muddy:

\[\neg \textsf{K}_a p_1 \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_a p_2 \wedge \neg \textsf{K}_a p_3\]

This formula is true at \(101\), since \(a\) cannot rule out \(001\) and \(c\) cannot rule out \(100\). It is false at every state with exactly one muddy child, since such a child sees no mud on any other child and, after \(A\), must know that they are muddy. The update removes those states, which is exactly the transition from the second to the third diagram.

9.3 Common Knowledge

What do the children learn after the first announcement? Notice that they all know that at least one child is muddy even prior to the announcement. We may use \(E\varphi\) as an abbreviation for the conjunction: \[\bigwedge_{i\in I} \textsf{K}_i \varphi,\]

which is read: “it is generally known that \(\varphi\)’’. General knowledge is not sufficient for common knowledge. A group has common knowledge that \(\varphi\) if they all know that \(\varphi\), they all know that they all know that \(\varphi\), they all know that they all know that they all know that \(\varphi\), etc.

Let us revisit the initial model for the Muddy Children Puzzle and let \(p_1\), \(p_2\), and \(p_3\) be true at exactly states at which each of \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), respectively, are muddy, then we may express the fact that at least one child is muddy by means of the disjunction \(p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3\). Notice that that formula is true when evaluated at state \(101\): \[M, 101 \Vdash \textsf{E} (p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3).\]

That is because: \[M, 101 \Vdash \textsf{K}_a (p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3) \wedge \textsf{K}_b (p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3) \wedge \textsf{K}_c (p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3).\]

But although it is generally known that at least one child is muddy, it is not common knowledge that at least one child is muddy.

Common knowledge plays a crucial role in social interaction. When we drive, for example, we rely on the fact that it is common knowledge that a red light requires drivers to stop. It is not sufficient for a pedestrian to know that, but it is important to know that drivers know that and for them to know that everyone knows that, etc.

How should we incorporate common knowledge in the language of epistemic logic? There is no hope for an explicit definition of common knowledge in terms of knowledge, for that would presumably require an infinite conjunction, which is not available in the language. We will instead expand the language of epistemic logic with a common knowledge operator, \(\textsf{C}\varphi\), read: “it is common knowledge that \(\varphi\).

Once we do this, we must explain how to evaluate a formula of the form \(\textsf{C}\varphi\) at a state \(s\) of a model \(M\) of the form \((W, \{R_i: i\in I\}, V)\). To that purpose, we will expand the definition of truth at a state with a clause for the newly added common knowledge operator.

  • a state \(t\) is reachable from \(s\) in one step, in symbols, \(sR^1t\), iff \(sR_it\) for some \(i\in I\). In the case at hand, one state is reachable from another in one step if it is \(a\)-accessible from the other or it is \(b\)-accessible from the other or, finally, it is \(c\)-accessible from the other.

  • a state \(t\) is reachable from \(s\) in \(n+1\) steps, in symbols, \(sR^{n+1}t\), iff for some step \(u\), \(sR^{n}u\) and \(uR_it\) for some \(i\in I\).

For purposes of illustration, notice that in the initial model for the Muddy Children Puzzle \(000\) is reachable from \(101\) in two steps: \[101 R_c 100 \ \ \text{and} \ \ 100 R_a 000\]

\[101 R_a 001 \ \ \text{and} \ \ 001 R_c 000\]

That is, there are two-step paths one may take from \(101\) to \(000\).

Finally,

  • a state \(t\) is reachable from \(s\) in a finite number of steps, in symbols \(R^\star\), iff there is some \(n\) such that \(t\) is reachable from \(s\) in \(n\) steps.

Here is the clause for \(\textsf{C}\varphi\) in the definition of truth at a state: \[\begin{array}{lll} M, s \Vdash \textsf{C} \varphi & \text{iff} & \text{for every} \ u \in W \ \text{s.t.} \ R^\star su, \ M, u \Vdash \varphi\\ \end{array}\]

In other words, \(\textsf{C}\varphi\) is true at a state \(s\) iff \(\varphi\) is true at every state \(t\) reachable from \(s\) in a finite number of steps.

Let us revisit the model for the Muddy Children Puzzle prior to the first announcement:

Before Announcement A

When we apply the definition of truth at a state to the case at hand, we find: \[M, 101 \nVdash \textsf{C} (p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3).\]

The reason is that the state \(000\), which falsifies the disjunction, is reachable from \(101\) in two steps.

The situation changes once the children eliminate that option after the announcement. One of the effects of the announcement in the Muddy Children Puzzle is to make it common knowledge that at least one child is muddy: it is not just that all children know that at least one child is muddy, all children now know that all children know, and all children know that all children know that they know that, etc.

After Announcement A

If we use \(M'\) to refer to the model that results, we find \[M', 101 \Vdash \textsf{C} (p_1 \vee p_2 \vee p_3).\]

This is because the disjunction is true at every state reachable from \(101\) in the new model.

After the first request is made without any children stepping forward, the children are able to rule out as epistemic alternatives states in which only one child is muddy.

After Request B

At this point, the fact that two children are muddy now becomes common knowledge, since only states where two children are muddy are reachable from the state of evaluation in a finite number of steps.

The actual distribution of mud becomes common knowledge after the two children step forward after the second request. That is because they are all left with a single epistemic live option:

After Request C

The accessibility relation for common knowledge, \(R^\star\), will of course be reflexive and transitive. It will be transitive by definition, but it will inherit reflexivity from the reflexivity of each of the \(i\)-accessibility relations. Whether the relation will in addition be symmetric or euclidean will crucially depend on the structure of the individual \(i\)-accessibility relations.

One axiomatization of the logic of common knowledge employs an an axiom and a rule of inference: \[\tag{Fixed Point} \textsf{C}p \to \textsf{E}(p \wedge \textsf{C}p)\] \[\tag{Induction} \varphi \to \textsf{E}(\psi \wedge \varphi) / \varphi \to \textsf{C} \psi\]

The fixed point axiom regards common knowledge as a fixed point of the function that maps a formula \(\varphi\) to \(\textsf{K}_i(\varphi \wedge \textsf{C}\varphi)\). The rule of inference is called an induction rule because the antecedent provides the crucial ingredient for an inductive argument for all instances of the schema: \[\varphi \to \textsf{E}^n(\psi \wedge \varphi),\] which guarantees the conditional \[\varphi \to \textsf{C}\psi.\]

For suppose the conditional \(\varphi \to \textsf{E}(\psi \wedge \varphi)\) is valid. Then if \(\varphi\) is true at \(s\), \(\textsf{E}(\psi \wedge \varphi)\) is true at \(s\), which means that if \(t\) is reachable from \(s\) in one step, then \(\varphi\) is true at \(t\). So, \(\textsf{E}(\psi \wedge \varphi)\) is true at \(t\). If \(u\) is now reachable from \(t\) in one step, then \(\textsf{E}(\psi \wedge \varphi)\) will be true at \(t\), etc.

We are in a position to prove some theorems of the logic of common knowledge.

Proposition 9.1 \(\vdash \textsf{C}p \to \textsf{E} \textsf{C}p\)

Proof. This is a simple consequence of the Fixed Point axiom: \[\begin{array}{lllll} 1 & & \textsf{C}p \to \textsf{E}(p \wedge \textsf{C}p) & \text{Fixed Point}\\ 2 & & \textsf{E}(p \wedge \textsf{C}p) \to (\textsf{E} p \wedge \textsf{E} \textsf{C}p) & \textsf{K} \ 1\\ 3 & & \textsf{C}p \to \textsf{E} \textsf{C}p & \textsf{PL} \ 1, 2\\ \end{array}\]

Proposition 9.2 \(\vdash \textsf{C}p\to \textsf{C C}p\)

Proof. This is a simple consequence of the rule of inference for common knowledge. \[\begin{array}{lllll} 1 & & \textsf{C}p \to \textsf{E} \textsf{C}p & \\ 2 & & \textsf{C} p \to (\textsf{C}p \wedge \textsf{C} p) & \textsf{PL} \\ 3 & & \textsf{EC} p \to \textsf{E} (\textsf{C}p\wedge \textsf{C} p) & \textsf{PL}\\ 4 & & \textsf{C} p \to \textsf{E} (\textsf{C}p \wedge \textsf{C} p) & \textsf{PL} \ 1, 3\\ 5 & & \textsf{C} p \to \textsf{CC}p & \textsf{RC}[\textsf{C}p/\varphi, \textsf{C}p/\psi] \ 4 \end{array}\]
